How the peg pyramid works

The board is a triangle of staggered pegs, with each row wider than the one above it. A ball dropped at the top has a roughly equal chance of moving left or right at every peg, repeating that split until it reaches the bottom. The result is one of the pockets along the base, with one more pocket than the number of rows.

Because every path is a chain of 50/50 turns, most balls drift toward the middle and only a few reach the edges. Mathematically, it is a Galton board, and the shape of outcomes follows a binomial curve. In practice, the payout table simply mirrors that curve: common pockets pay less, while rare edge pockets pay more in casino thorfortune style games.

Straight answers to five questions

Is every drop truly random?

On licensed platforms, yes. Each deflection comes from an audited random number generator, and provably fair versions allow you to verify later that the result was set before the ball moved.

Do more rows improve the payout?

No. More rows add pockets and stretch the odds curve, so edge results become rarer while central ones remain common. The return stays broadly the same across board sizes.

Which risk level is the better choice?

None is mathematically superior. Low risk smooths the ride, while high risk places more value in rare edge hits. The right setting depends on your budget and tolerance for swings.
